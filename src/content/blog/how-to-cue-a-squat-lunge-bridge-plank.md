To cue a squat in a group class, name the Movement, give one set-up cue, give one action cue, and say all of it two to four beats before the room moves. "Squat. Feet hip-width, toes slightly out. Sit back like there's a chair behind you." Then watch, and correct one thing at a time. The same pattern works for a lunge, a glute bridge and a plank.

The hard part of cueing a sculpt class is not knowing what good form looks like. It is fitting the right five words into the two seconds you have before the next rep, over music, to a room of 20 people at different levels. This post is the word-for-word version: what we say for four Movements we put in nearly every sculpt Flow, when we say it, and what we say when it goes wrong.

## The cueing pattern we use

SilverSneakers' instructor training gives a clean four-layer model in its [lesson on cueing for coaching](https://ircwp.tivityhealth.com/course/silversneakers-fundamentals-of-teaching-group-fitness/lessons/unit-2-effective-cueing/topic/lesson-2-3-cueing-vs-coaching/). We use a version of it:

1. **Name it.** "Squat." One word, on the beat.
2. **Set it up.** One to three cues for position, from the ground up.
3. **Refine it.** One correction at a time, said positively.
4. **Give it a reason, then motivate.** "This is the strength you use getting out of a chair." "Four more."

The same lesson makes two points we would underline. Cue with as few words as possible, four to two beats ahead of the change. And say what to do, not what not to do: "knees follow your toes" lands better than "don't let your knees cave".

ACE's [Cued Up for Success](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/everyone/blog/8484/cued-up-for-success/) sorts cues into three jobs: performance cues (how to do it), safety cues (how not to get hurt) and alerting cues (what is about to change). In a sculpt class you need all three, but the alerting cue is the one new instructors forget. "Last 8, then hold" is a cue. So is "Lunges next, step your right foot back on 1."

### How early is "four beats ahead"?

Not long. Here is the time you have at common sculpt tempos:

| BPM | 2 beats | 4 beats | One eight-count |
|---|---|---|---|
| 116 | 1.0 s | 2.1 s | 4.1 s |
| 124 | 1.0 s | 1.9 s | 3.9 s |
| 128 | 0.9 s | 1.9 s | 3.75 s |

Two seconds is about five or six short words. That is why "Squat, sit back, chest proud" works and "OK, so next we're going to go into a squat, and I want you to think about" does not. If the music is faster than the cue, count the Movement in half time and give yourself an extra two beats. Our guide to [matching sculpt movements to BPM](/blog/matching-sculpt-movements-to-bpm/) covers half, single and double time.

### Point the cue outside the body

Many of the cues below talk about the floor, a chair or a direction in the room rather than a muscle. That is deliberate. Gabriele Wulf's [review of 15 years of attentional focus research](https://gwulf.faculty.unlv.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Wulf_AF_review_2013.pdf) found that directing attention to the effect of a movement (an external focus) generally helps people perform and learn it better than focusing on their own body movements. In class, "push the floor away" usually beats "squeeze your quads".

## How to cue a squat

**Set-up (say it once, before the first rep):** "Feet hip-width, toes slightly out. Weight in the whole foot."

**Action:** "Sit back like there's a chair behind you. Push the floor away to stand."

**On the beat:** Teach the first 8 reps in half time, "down 2, up 2", so people can find the shape. Then single time ("down, up"), then pulses at the bottom. This is the layering used across our [sculpt sequence ideas](/blog/sculpt-sequence-ideas/).

**Fixes, one at a time:**

| What you see | What to say |
|---|---|
| Knees drifting in | "Knees follow your second toe." Or, external: "Spread the floor apart with your feet." |
| Heels lifting | "Whole foot down. Wiggle your toes." |
| Chest dropping towards the thighs | "Chest proud. Show the front of your top to the mirror." |
| Going deeper than they can control | "Lower only as far as you can still see your toes in front of your knees." |
| Holding their breath | "Breathe out as you stand." |

That depth cue is adapted from AFAA's guide to [inclusive group fitness cues](https://blog.afaa.com/group-fitness-cues), which suggests replacing "as low as you can go" with a limit everyone can check for themselves. It works especially well for sumo squats and pliés, where people tend to chase depth.

**Variations:** For a sumo squat, add "heels in, toes out, knees open over the toes". For a goblet squat, add "weight at your chest, elbows point down".

## How to cue a lunge and a curtsy lunge

We teach the reverse lunge before the forward lunge. Stepping back keeps the weight over the front foot, which is easier to control at speed than stepping forward and braking.

**Reverse lunge set-up:** "Feet under your hips. Right foot steps back on 1."

**Action:** "Both knees bend. Back heel lifted. Push through the front foot to stand."

**Depth:** We use a version of AFAA's inclusive lunge cue: "Lower your back knee as far as you can while your spine stays long." It lets someone with tight hip flexors stop short of the floor without arching their back.

### How to cue a curtsy lunge

The curtsy lunge needs more cueing than most lower-body Movements, because it asks for balance on a narrow base while the back leg crosses behind.

**Set-up:** "Right foot steps back and across, behind your left heel. Think of your back foot reaching for seven o'clock."

**Action:** "Hips face the front. Front knee follows the front toes. Back heel lifted. Stand up through the front foot."

**Fixes:**

| What you see | What to say |
|---|---|
| Hips twisting towards the back leg | "Both hip bones face the front of the room, like headlights." |
| Front knee collapsing inward | "Front knee over your middle toes." |
| Wobbling or falling sideways | "Smaller step. Hand on the wall, chair or barre if you need it." |
| Back knee slamming down | "Lower with control. Back knee hovers." |

**Instead of curtsy lunges:** a reverse lunge, a lateral lunge, a split squat holding a chair or barre, or a "curtsy tap-back" with no weight, where the back toes just tap behind and come back. Offer one of these out loud, not as a whisper to one person, so nobody feels singled out for taking it.

## How to cue a glute bridge

NASM's [glute bridge coaching guide](https://www.nasm.org/resource-center/blog/training/why-the-glute-bridge-belongs-in-almost-every-client-program) puts the heels about 6 to 8 inches from the glutes, feet hip-width with toes forward, and has clients brace, then lift until there is a straight line from knees to shoulders.

**Set-up:** "On your back, knees bent. Feet hip-width, heels about a hand's length from your seat."

**Action:** "Press through your heels and lift your hips. Knees, hips, shoulders in one line. Lower slowly."

**On the beat:** "Up 2, down 2" for 8 reps. Then hold at the top and pulse an inch for 16. Then hold for an eight-count. Add a ball between the knees or a band above them for the second set.

**Fixes:**

| What you see | What to say |
|---|---|
| "I feel it in my hamstrings" | "Walk your feet an inch closer to your seat." NASM gives the same fix. |
| Lower back arching at the top | "Lift with your hips, not your back." (NASM's wording.) Then: "Ribs soft towards the floor." |
| Knees falling open | "Knees stay hip-width, pointing at the ceiling." |
| Pushing through the toes | "Lift your toes. Heels do the work." |

## How to cue a plank

**Set-up:** "Hands under shoulders. Step back one foot, then the other."

**Action:** "Push the floor away. Heels reach back. Hold."

**Fixes:**

| What you see | What to say |
|---|---|
| Hips sagging | "Lift your hips to the height of your shoulders." |
| Hips piked high | "Lower your hips until shoulders, hips and heels line up." |
| Shoulders sinking towards the ears | "Push the floor away. Make space between your shoulders and ears." |
| Shaking hard at 20 seconds | "Knees down, keep the line from knees to head. Same work, less lever." |

For a side plank, start with the bottom knee down: "Elbow under shoulder. Bottom knee down, top leg long. Lift your hip to the ceiling." Offer the full side plank only after everyone has found the supported version.

## Cues to cut

Most cueing problems are extra words, not missing ones. In IDEA's [Group Exercise Cues for Success](https://www.ideafit.com/group-exercise-cues-for-success/), fitness educator Keli Roberts points out that "lift it and squeeze it" and "that leg" leave people guessing which arm or leg you mean, and suggests filming yourself teaching to catch your own filler words. Here is what we edit out:

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "Next we're going to go into a squat" | "Squat." |
| "Lift it and squeeze it" | "Lift your right leg. Squeeze your seat." |
| "Don't arch your back" | "Ribs soft towards the floor." |
| "Go as low as you can" | "Lower until you can still see your toes." |
| "OK, a few more of those" | "Last 8." |

## How to cue: quick answers

**How do you cue a squat in a group class?** Name it, give one set-up cue from the feet up, then one action cue, and say it two to four beats before the room moves: "Squat. Feet hip-width, toes slightly out. Sit back like there's a chair behind you." Then correct one thing at a time.

**What are some good cues for squats?** "Sit back like there's a chair behind you", "knees follow your second toe", "whole foot on the floor", "chest proud, eyes to the front of the room" and "push the floor away to stand". Pick one per set, not all five.

**How do you cue a lunge?** Cue the step, then the depth, then the drive: "Step back, both knees bend, back heel lifted. Lower until it feels strong, not as far as you can. Push through the front foot to stand."

**What can you do instead of curtsy lunges?** A reverse lunge, a lateral lunge, a split squat holding a barre or chair, or a curtsy tap-back with no weight. All of them work the legs and glutes with an easier balance demand.

**Why can't I feel my glutes in a glute bridge?** Often the feet are too far from the hips, so the hamstrings take over. NASM suggests heels about 6 to 8 inches from the glutes and moving the feet slightly closer if the hamstrings dominate.

If someone in your room has pain during any of these Movements, rather than ordinary effort, stop the Movement and suggest they check with a qualified professional.
